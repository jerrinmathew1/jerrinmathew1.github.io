Sleep Deprivation and Mood: Why Chicago Professionals Need to Take Sleep Seriously

For Chicago and Illinois professionals seeking psychiatric care, understanding your options and what to expect helps you make informed decisions about treatment.

Who this is for: Professionals in Chicago and across Illinois dealing with low mood, exhaustion, or burnout. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Sleep is often the first thing a busy professional gives up and the last thing they suspect when their mood changes. Yet short sleep is one of the most consistent and most modifiable risk factors for depression and anxiety. Treating sleep is frequently one of the most effective things a person can do for their mental health.

What Sleep Loss Does to Emotion

Sleep, and dreaming sleep in particular, helps the brain process emotional experiences. When sleep is cut short, the brain's threat-detection system becomes more reactive while the frontal regions that normally calm it work less effectively (Goldstein & Walker, 2014). In daily life, that looks like a shorter fuse, more worry, and small problems that feel large.

How Short Sleep Raises the Risk of Depression

Insomnia is not only a symptom of depression. It often comes first. A meta-analysis of long-term studies found that people with insomnia had more than twice the risk of developing depression later compared with people who slept well (Baglioni et al., 2011).

Why You Stop Noticing

In a carefully controlled laboratory study, people limited to six hours of sleep a night for two weeks developed attention and memory deficits comparable to going one or two full nights without sleep. Over the same period, they rated themselves as only slightly sleepy (Van Dongen et al., 2003). In other words, chronic short sleep impairs performance while hiding the impairment from the person experiencing it.

How Much Is Enough

Sleep medicine experts recommend that adults regularly sleep seven or more hours a night for good health (Watson et al., 2015). Some people need more. Very few people function at their best on less over the long term, however much they may believe they do.

Sleep as Part of Psychiatric Treatment

When someone has depression or anxiety, sleep deserves its own attention. Questions worth asking include:

  • Is the problem falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early?
  • Are there signs of a sleep disorder such as sleep apnea, restless legs, or a circadian rhythm problem?
  • Are caffeine, alcohol, cannabis, or medications playing a role?
  • Is work, including shift work or late-night email, leaving too little time for sleep at all?

For chronic insomnia, cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia is the recommended first treatment and usually works better over time than sleeping pills.

Habits That Help

  • Keep the same wake time every day, including weekends.
  • Get daylight early in the day, and keep evenings dim.
  • Stop caffeine at least eight hours before bed.
  • Use alcohol sparingly. It helps you fall asleep but fragments sleep later in the night.
  • Keep the bedroom cool, dark, and quiet, and keep work out of it.

From the Bedside

I work nights in a neuro ICU, so I know how easy it is to accept exhaustion as part of the job. I have also seen, in myself and in colleagues, how much harder everything feels on four hours of sleep: the patience that runs out, the worry that will not quiet down, the small mistake that would never happen on a rested day. Protecting sleep is not a luxury for people with easy schedules. It is basic maintenance for anyone who needs their judgment to be reliable.

When to Get Help

If poor sleep has lasted more than a month, or comes with low mood, constant worry, or loud snoring and daytime sleepiness, it is worth an evaluation. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
