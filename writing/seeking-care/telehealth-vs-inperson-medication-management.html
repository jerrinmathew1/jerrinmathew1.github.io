Medication Management via Telehealth Versus In-Person in Illinois

For Chicago and Illinois professionals seeking psychiatric care, understanding your options and what to expect helps you make informed decisions about treatment.

Who this is for: People in Chicago and across Illinois trying to find the right mental health care. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Psychiatric medication can be managed by video or in an office, and for most people in Illinois the choice comes down to what each visit needs to accomplish. Some visits depend on a blood pressure cuff, a scale, or a clinician watching how you walk across a room. Many others are conversations about sleep, mood, side effects, and how the week went, and those translate to video well.

The research on this question is more reassuring than many people expect, and it points toward a practical answer: match the format to the job.

What the Research Shows

Reviews spanning several decades of studies have found that psychiatric care delivered by video produces outcomes comparable to in-person care for diagnosis and treatment across a wide range of conditions, with high satisfaction among patients and clinicians (Hilty et al., 2013; Bashshur et al., 2016). Structured interviews done over video agree closely with the same interviews done face to face (Chakrabarti, 2015).

In one well-known randomized trial in rural primary care clinics, people with depression whose care was coordinated through telemedicine had better response and remission rates than those receiving the same model of care on site (Fortney et al., 2013). The lesson is not that video is better. It is that access and consistency matter, and video can make it easier to keep appointments that would otherwise be missed.

What an In-Person Visit Adds

Medication management is not only talk. Some medications call for measurements and observations that a camera cannot make:

  • Vital signs and weight. Stimulants can raise heart rate and blood pressure. Several antipsychotics and mood stabilizers affect weight, blood sugar, and cholesterol.
  • Movement. Tremor, restlessness, stiffness, or unusual movements are easier to judge in person.
  • A fuller picture. When the diagnosis is uncertain, or medical problems may be driving symptoms, an exam and coordinated lab work help.
  • Safety. Acute crises, severe depression with suicidal thoughts, or symptoms of mania or psychosis are better handled in person, and sometimes urgently.

What Video Does Well

  • Follow-up visits. Checking how a medication is working, adjusting a dose, and talking through side effects.
  • Consistency. No commute means fewer canceled visits during busy seasons at work.
  • Reach. People anywhere in Illinois can see the same clinician, not only those near downtown Chicago.
  • Measurement. Validated questionnaires such as the PHQ-9 for depression work just as well on a screen (Kroenke et al., 2001), and a home blood pressure cuff can fill in the vital signs.

A Hybrid Plan Is Often Best

Many people do well with an in-person evaluation, especially when a medication with cardiac or metabolic effects is being started, followed by video visits once things are stable. A switch back to in person makes sense when symptoms change suddenly, a new medical problem appears, or a medication needs a closer look.

From the Bedside

Most of my career has been spent at the bedside: first on an ambulance, then in skilled nursing, and now in intensive care, where a set of vital signs can change a plan in minutes. That experience makes me respect what an exam adds. It has also shown me how often the biggest obstacle to good care is simply getting to it: the missed follow-up, the prescription that runs out, the appointment that falls apart because of traffic or a late shift. Video does not replace a careful exam. For routine follow-up, it removes many of the reasons people fall out of care.

When to Get Help

If you are unsure which format fits, ask the clinician directly what they need to see in person and why. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
