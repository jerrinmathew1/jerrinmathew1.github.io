When perfectionism becomes a liability

High standards produce extraordinary work. Perfectionism produces delay, and they are not the same thing.

This article addresses concerns common among Chicago perfectionist culture, and reflects both clinical evidence and direct experience in psychiatric practice.

JM
Jerrin Mathew, RN, BSN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner student

High standards produce extraordinary work. A great deal of what is best in medicine, law, engineering, and design exists because someone refused to accept something adequate.

Perfectionism is a different thing wearing the same clothes, and it becomes expensive in ways that are hard to see from inside.

What it costs

A perfectionistic professional may spend six hours improving something that was already good after two. They may struggle to delegate, because nobody else will do it correctly, and then be overwhelmed by the volume they have retained.

They may avoid risk, since anything genuinely new carries a real chance of being done badly. They may delay projects past the point where the delay costs more than the imperfection would have. And they may experience distress over ordinary mistakes that is entirely disproportionate to the consequences.

The distinction that matters

The critical question is not how high the standards are. It is whether they remain flexible.

Healthy striving sounds like: this is important, so I will do my best work on it. It permits calibration — more effort on the things that matter, less on the things that do not.

Perfectionism sounds like: if this is not perfect, I have failed. It applies the same standard to a board presentation and an internal email, because the standard is not really about the work.

Healthy striving is about the output. Perfectionism is about what the output would say about you.

The hidden ledger

Perfectionism consumes time, attention, creativity, sleep, relationships, and willingness to experiment. None of those costs appear on any professional metric, which is why the trait is so often described as an asset.

It also reinforces anxiety and avoidance. If starting something means committing to producing something flawless, starting becomes considerably harder, and postponement becomes the rational short-term move.

Why it limits senior careers specifically

The trait that works well in junior roles works badly in senior ones, and the transition catches people out.

Leadership requires delegation, which requires tolerating work done to someone else's standard. Innovation requires experimentation, which requires tolerating failure. Developing people requires letting them make mistakes that you could have prevented.

A professional who cannot tolerate imperfection tends to hit a ceiling not because they lack ability but because the next role requires a capacity they have never developed.

When it is worth clinical attention

Perfectionism is not a diagnosis, and most perfectionistic people do not need treatment.

It becomes worth attention when it is producing significant distress, when it is accompanied by anxiety or low mood, when checking and rechecking consume substantial time, or when the fear of error has begun determining what someone is willing to attempt.

It is also worth noting where the boundaries lie. Obsessive-compulsive disorder involves intrusive thoughts and compulsions that are difficult to control and cause meaningful distress or impairment — a distinct condition rather than a more intense version of perfectionism. Perfectionism also appears frequently alongside eating disorders and anxiety disorders.

What changing it does not require

People resist addressing perfectionism because they believe the alternative is not caring, and not caring is unthinkable.

That is not the trade. The aim is the ability to decide how good something needs to be, deliberately, based on what it is for — and then to stop. Standards stay. The rigidity goes.

Medical disclaimerPerfectionism is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis. It can occur as a personality trait and can also accompany anxiety disorders, depression, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and eating disorders. This article is educational and is not a substitute for professional evaluation.
