OCD Is Not About Being Tidy

"I am a bit OCD about my desk" has done real damage to people who actually have it.

Who this is for: Adults in Chicago and across Illinois living with intrusive thoughts or compulsions. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

The casual use of the term has made obsessive-compulsive disorder one of the most misunderstood conditions in psychiatry. Many people with OCD are not tidy at all. Many tidy people do not have OCD. The disorder is not a preference for order; it is a cycle of intrusive distress and the behaviors performed to neutralize it.

What It Actually Is

Obsessions are intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that cause marked distress. Compulsions are behaviors or mental acts performed to reduce that distress or prevent a feared outcome. The compulsion brings brief relief, which teaches the brain that the threat was real, and the cycle tightens.

The National Institute of Mental Health notes that the time consumed and the distress caused are central — people with OCD typically recognize that the thoughts are excessive and still cannot stop responding to them.

The Forms People Do Not Recognize

  • Harm obsessions: intrusive images of hurting someone, especially someone loved, in people who are horrified by them
  • Checking: doors, emails, calculations, prescriptions — repeatedly, past any reasonable certainty
  • Moral and religious scrupulosity: persistent fear of having done something wrong or sinful
  • Relationship OCD: relentless doubt about whether one loves one's partner
  • Purely mental compulsions: reviewing, reassuring oneself, neutralizing thoughts — invisible to anyone watching

The forms with no visible compulsion are the ones most often missed for years.

Why It Goes Undiagnosed

Shame, mostly. People with intrusive harm or sexual thoughts often believe that saying them aloud will reveal them as dangerous, so they say nothing. The average delay from onset to treatment has historically been long.

The intrusive thoughts in OCD are distressing precisely because they run against what the person values. That is a defining feature, not a warning sign.

It Is Very Treatable

Exposure and response prevention and certain medications both have strong evidence. Getting the diagnosis right is the step that makes treatment possible.

What the Research Shows

OCD affects about 2 to 3% of people over their lifetime, and most also experience other conditions, especially anxiety and depression (Ruscio et al., 2010). It usually begins in adolescence or early adulthood (Pinto et al., 2006). The good news is that treatment works. Cognitive behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention produces large improvements in controlled trials (Öst et al., 2015), and SSRI medications are also effective, alone or alongside therapy (Skapinakis et al., 2016).

From the Bedside

Healthcare runs on hand hygiene and checklists, so I have seen the line between careful and compulsive from close range. Careful habits serve a purpose and then stop. Compulsions keep going because they never quite deliver the certainty they promise. When someone describes rituals they cannot stop, doubts that never settle, or thoughts that horrify them, the word "tidy" does not come close to capturing it.

When to Get Help

If obsessions or compulsions take up more than an hour a day or cause significant distress, ask for an assessment by someone experienced with OCD. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
