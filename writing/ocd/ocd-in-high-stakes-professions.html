OCD in High-Stakes Professions

In some professions, the compulsions look exactly like being good at the job.

Who this is for: Adults in Chicago and across Illinois living with intrusive thoughts or compulsions. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

In most settings, compulsive checking stands out. In medicine, law, aviation, and finance it can be hard to distinguish from professional diligence. Double-checking a dose is good practice. Checking it eleven times, then lying awake checking it in your head, is something else.

Where the Line Sits

Conscientiousness is proportionate and flexible. You check, you are satisfied, you move on. OCD is disproportionate and unsatisfiable. Each check brings brief relief and then the doubt returns, and the threshold for certainty keeps rising.

The telling features are time consumed, distress caused, and the inability to stop despite recognizing that it is excessive.

How It Often Looks

  • A physician re-reviewing completed notes or orders well after the shift ends
  • An attorney unable to send a document without repeated rereading for errors that are not there
  • Intrusive fears of having harmed a patient through an error that did not happen
  • Persistent doubt about past decisions and the need to reconstruct them to be sure
  • Avoiding responsibilities that trigger the doubt, which then looks like underperformance

The person is often regarded as the most careful one on the team, while privately losing hours a day.

Why Professionals Hide It

Fear that disclosing intrusive harm thoughts will be misread as danger to patients or clients. Fear of what it means for licensing. And because the behavior is professionally rewarded, it can go years without anyone else seeing a problem.

Why Treatment Matters Here

Untreated, OCD in these roles tends to grow, consuming time and producing exhaustion and avoidance. Treated, people commonly retain the genuine strengths — thoroughness, care — while losing the hours of checking and the constant doubt.

What the Research Shows

About 2 to 3% of people will have OCD at some point in their lives, and most also experience another condition such as anxiety or depression (Ruscio et al., 2010). Symptoms most often begin in the teens or early twenties (Pinto et al., 2006). Effective treatment exists: cognitive behavioral therapy with exposure and response prevention produces large improvements in controlled trials (Öst et al., 2015), and a network meta-analysis found that both psychological treatment and SSRI medication are effective, with exposure-based therapy among the strongest options (Skapinakis et al., 2016).

From the Bedside

Medicine depends on checklists, double checks, and a healthy fear of mistakes. I rely on them every shift. What I have learned is that useful checking has an end: you verify, you are done, and you move on. OCD checking does not end, because the doubt is not really about the task. People in high-stakes jobs often hide that difference for years, believing it is simply what being careful means. It is not, and it is treatable.

When to Get Help

If checking, doubting, or mental reviewing is costing you an hour or more a day, or causing real distress, an assessment is worthwhile. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
