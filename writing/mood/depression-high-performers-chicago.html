Depression in High-Achieving Chicago Professionals: What It Looks Like and How to Get Help

For Chicago and Illinois professionals seeking psychiatric care, understanding your options and what to expect helps you make informed decisions about treatment.

Who this is for: Professionals in Chicago and across Illinois dealing with low mood, exhaustion, or burnout. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Depression in high-achieving professionals often looks nothing like the stereotype. You still show up. You still deliver. From the outside, very little seems wrong. Inside, things feel flat, effortful, or empty, and the gap between how you appear and how you feel can become its own kind of exhaustion.

How Depression Hides in High Performers

  • Working through it. Deadlines are still met, but nothing about the accomplishment registers.
  • Loss of pleasure. Hobbies, friends, and vacations feel like obligations rather than relief.
  • Exhaustion that rest does not fix. A weekend off changes nothing.
  • Harsh self-criticism. Success feels undeserved, and every mistake feels like proof of failure.
  • Quiet withdrawal. Engaged at work, absent everywhere else.
  • Physical symptoms. Changes in sleep, appetite, energy, and concentration that are easy to blame on workload.

Why It Is Easy to Miss

Most of the cost of depression at work does not come from missed days. It comes from showing up while unwell. A study across eight countries found that lost productivity from working while depressed, often called presenteeism, far outweighed the cost of absences (Evans-Lacko & Knapp, 2016). Depression is consistently linked with reduced concentration, slower decision-making, and difficulty with interpersonal demands at work (Lerner & Henke, 2008). High performers tend to compensate by working longer, which hides the problem until it no longer can be hidden.

How It Is Assessed

A good evaluation looks at mood, sleep, energy, appetite, concentration, and safety, and asks about medical conditions, medications, alcohol, and substances that can cause or worsen depressive symptoms. Brief validated questionnaires such as the PHQ-9 help measure severity and track change over time (Kroenke et al., 2001). Ruling out other explanations, such as thyroid disease, sleep apnea, or bipolar disorder, matters because the right treatment depends on the right diagnosis.

What Treatment Can Look Like

Depression is very treatable. Psychotherapy and medication are each effective, and combining them tends to work better than either alone, particularly for moderate to severe depression (Cuijpers et al., 2020). Practical changes matter too: protecting sleep, restoring exercise and time with people, and reducing alcohol. Improvement is often gradual, and the first sign is frequently that sleep or energy changes before mood does.

From the Bedside

On a cardiac unit, I cared for many people whose depression showed up only after a medical crisis forced them to stop. Some later said they had been running on empty for years and had never named it. High performers are often the last people anyone checks on, including themselves. Naming depression is not an admission of weakness. It is the first step toward getting back the parts of your life that have gone quiet.

When to Get Help

If low mood, loss of interest, or exhaustion have lasted more than two weeks and are affecting your work or relationships, it is time to talk to a clinician. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
