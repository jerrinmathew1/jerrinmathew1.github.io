Who this is for

People already receiving psychiatric care who want an independent look at it. You are not necessarily unhappy with your clinician. You may simply have questions that a fifteen-minute medication check has never had room for.

Common reasons people ask for one:

  • A diagnosis was made years ago, quickly, and never revisited
  • You have been on the same regimen for a long time without a clear rationale ever being explained
  • You have tried several medications without adequate response and want to know what has actually been ruled out
  • You suspect something was missed — ADHD underneath the anxiety, bipolar spectrum underneath the depression, sleep disorder underneath everything
  • You are considering a change and want an independent view before making it

What happens

  1. Records review. You send whatever you have — prior notes, medication history, testing, discharge summaries. I review it before we meet, on my time, not yours.
  2. Ninety-minute consultation. A full history and diagnostic assessment, not a review of your current prescription list. We go back to the beginning.
  3. A written summary. My diagnostic impression, what I think fits and what does not, and specific options worth discussing — written in language you can hand to your own clinician.

What this is not

This is not a transfer of care, and it is not an attempt to take you from your clinician. Most people leave with a document and return to the person already treating them, better equipped to have a specific conversation.

It is also not a prescription service. I will not prescribe or adjust medication from a one-time consultation, because that is not safe practice. Recommendations go to you and, with your consent, to your treating clinician.

If it turns out that ongoing care with me makes sense, that is a separate conversation and a separate evaluation.

Why the records review matters

Most second opinions are worth little because the clinician is working from a fifteen-minute summary given by a patient under time pressure. Reviewing the actual record beforehand is what makes the appointment useful — it means the ninety minutes goes to thinking rather than to reconstructing history.

That preparation is why this costs more than a standard initial evaluation despite being a single visit.

What you leave with

  • A written diagnostic formulation — what I think is going on and why
  • An explicit account of what does not fit, which is often the more useful half
  • Specific, named options worth considering, with the reasoning behind each
  • Where relevant, what testing or workup has not been done
