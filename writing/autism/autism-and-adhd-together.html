Autism and ADHD Together

For years they could not officially be diagnosed together. Clinically, they turn up together constantly.

Who this is for: Adults in Chicago and across Illinois who are autistic, or wonder whether they might be. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Until 2013, diagnostic criteria did not permit autism and ADHD to be diagnosed in the same person. Clinicians who saw both were obliged to choose. That rule has gone, and it is now well recognized that the two co-occur at substantial rates.

How They Pull Against Each Other

The combination is often described as internally contradictory. ADHD pulls toward novelty, stimulation, and spontaneity. Autism pulls toward routine, predictability, and sameness. A person with both may crave new input and be destabilized by change, need structure and be unable to maintain it.

People with both often describe feeling at war with their own preferences.

Why One Masks the Other

The autistic need for systems and routine can partially compensate for ADHD disorganization, so the ADHD looks milder than it is. The ADHD distractibility can make autistic traits look like inattention. Either diagnosis alone tends to explain part of the picture and leave a remainder nobody can account for.

Why It Matters Clinically

Stimulant medication for the ADHD component can help a great deal. It can also, in some autistic people, increase anxiety or sensory sensitivity, so it warrants careful titration and honest reporting.

Strategies built for ADHD alone — novelty, variety, gamified systems — can backfire when routine is what is actually needed. Strategies built for autism alone can underestimate how much executive dysfunction is present. Treatment works better when both are named.

What the Research Shows

Autism and ADHD occur together far more often than chance would predict. A large meta-analysis estimated that about 28% of autistic people also have ADHD, alongside high rates of anxiety and depression (Lai et al., 2019). Another meta-analysis focused on ADHD estimated a pooled prevalence of roughly 4 in 10 among autistic individuals (Rong et al., 2021). Since 2013, diagnostic guidelines have allowed both diagnoses to be made in the same person, which has made the overlap easier to recognize (Hours et al., 2022).

The two conditions share some features, such as difficulties with attention and emotional regulation, which makes careful assessment important. Treatment decisions, including whether and how to use ADHD medication, depend on understanding which difficulties come from which condition (Antshel & Russo, 2019).

From the Bedside

I have seen how often adults are given one explanation for everything, when two different things are actually happening at once. When a single diagnosis does not fully explain someone's experience, the right response is a closer look, not a stronger dose. A good assessment takes the time to separate the threads.

When to Get Help

If you have one of these diagnoses and the treatment has only partly helped, ask whether the other might also be present. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.

If This Sounds Familiar

If you have an ADHD diagnosis and treatment has helped but left something unexplained — social exhaustion, sensory overload, distress at change — it is a reasonable question to raise. The reverse applies equally.
