Masking: The Cost of Performing Normal at Work

Every meeting is two jobs: the meeting, and the performance of being someone who finds meetings easy.

Who this is for: Adults in Chicago and across Illinois who are autistic, or wonder whether they might be. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Masking describes the deliberate, often automatic, suppression of autistic traits in order to appear neurotypical: rehearsing conversations, forcing eye contact, mirroring other people's expressions, suppressing the need to move or to step away, and carefully monitoring tone.

It works, in the sense that people often succeed in not being noticed. That success is the problem.

What It Costs

Masking is continuous cognitive labor running underneath every interaction. People describe ending a workday with nothing left, not because the work was hard but because the performance was. Weekends become recovery rather than living.

Over years the cost compounds. Masking is consistently associated in research with exhaustion, anxiety, and depression, and with a sense of not knowing who one actually is beneath the performance.

Masking is not a social skill. It is a load, and loads accumulate.

Why Professionals Mask More

Professional environments reward exactly the behaviors masking produces: composure, social fluency, reading the room. The more senior the role, the more it depends on interaction, and the higher the masking demand. Promotion can be the event that tips someone from coping into burnout.

What Helps

Not abandoning masking entirely — that is rarely realistic in a professional role. Rather, reducing the total load: building recovery time into the week deliberately, adjusting the environment where possible, choosing where to spend the effort, and being unmasked somewhere.

Knowing that this is what is happening is often the first relief. Exhaustion with a cause is easier to manage than exhaustion that seems to prove something is wrong with you.

What the Research Shows

In interviews with autistic adults, researchers found that people camouflage to fit in, connect with others, and avoid negative attention, and that the costs include exhaustion, anxiety, and a sense of losing track of who they are (Hull et al., 2017). A questionnaire developed to measure camouflaging now makes it possible to study the effects more precisely (Hull et al., 2019). Camouflaging across many settings, rather than only some, has been linked with more stress (Cage & Troxell-Whitman, 2019), and in one study camouflaging was associated with suicidal thoughts and behavior (Cassidy et al., 2018).

None of this means masking is a personal failing. It is a reasonable response to environments that are often unwelcoming. The aim of care is to give people more choice about when they mask, and more places where they do not have to.

From the Bedside

Nursing, like many professions, rewards a calm and consistent outward presence regardless of what is happening inside. I have seen how tiring that performance can be by the end of a twelve-hour shift, even for people who are not autistic. For people who have to mask their basic way of moving through the world, the cost is higher and constant. Recognizing it is the first step toward reducing it.

When to Get Help

If masking at work is leaving you exhausted, anxious, or burned out, a clinician who understands autism can help you sort out what to change and what support to ask for. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
