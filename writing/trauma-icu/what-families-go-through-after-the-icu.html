After the ICU: What Families Go Through, and Why It Has a Name

The patient leaves the ICU. The family often carries it home.

For families of ICU patients in Chicago and Illinois: This article reflects clinical evidence and years at the ICU bedside. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Nov 2, 2026

In my years as an ICU nurse, some of the hardest moments I have seen were not at the bedside of the patient. They were in the family lounge at 3 a.m.: a spouse who had not slept in four days, a daughter making decisions she never expected to make, a parent memorizing every alarm on the monitor. When the patient finally leaves the ICU, everyone expects the family to feel relief. Many do. Many also feel something they cannot explain.

It Has a Name: PICS-F

Critical care specialists call it post-intensive care syndrome-family, or PICS-F: new or worse anxiety, depression, trauma symptoms, sleep problems, or grief in family members after a loved one's critical illness. The term was introduced in 2012 to give families' experience the same attention as the patient's recovery.

It is common. Estimates vary widely because studies measure it differently, but many report symptoms in somewhere between a third and half of family members, and far more when the patient dies or when families make end-of-life decisions without enough support. Symptoms can begin in the ICU and last months, sometimes years.

What It Looks Like

  • Replaying alarms, conversations, or the moment you got the phone call
  • Nightmares, or lying awake listening for something to go wrong
  • Avoiding the hospital, the drive there, or anything that brings it back
  • Constant worry about a relapse, and checking on your loved one again and again
  • Guilt about decisions you made, or did not make, during the ICU stay
  • Exhaustion, irritability, and trouble concentrating at work
  • Low mood that settles in after the crisis is over, when everyone assumes you are fine

The patient may be home and recovering. That does not mean the family has recovered.

Who Is Most at Risk

Research points to the same patterns I see at the bedside. Family members are more likely to struggle afterward when they:

  • are the spouse or partner, or the main caregiver at home
  • had anxiety, depression, or trauma before the ICU stay
  • had to make major decisions on the patient's behalf
  • felt shut out, by restricted visiting or by too little communication from the care team
  • lost their loved one in the ICU
  • went home to heavy caregiving with little help

What Helps During the ICU Stay

  • Ask for a family meeting. A scheduled conversation with the team about the plan, the prognosis, and what to expect. Bring a notebook and one other person.
  • Be present when you can. Most ICUs now welcome families at the bedside, including during rounds. Being there, and understanding what you see, reduces fear.
  • Ask nurses what the machines mean. Not knowing is often worse than knowing. We would rather explain an alarm than have you sit with it alone.
  • Keep a simple diary. Dates, what happened, who you spoke to. It helps you track a blur of days, and later helps the patient understand time they cannot remember.
  • Take shifts. Sleep, eat, and step outside. A family that burns out in week one has nothing left for the recovery.

What Helps After

If symptoms last more than a month after the ICU stay, or get worse instead of better, take them seriously. That is the point at which clinicians usually look for PICS-F, and it is treatable.

  • Tell your own doctor. Your primary care clinician can screen for anxiety, depression, and trauma symptoms and refer you.
  • Consider trauma-focused therapy. Cognitive behavioral approaches have good evidence for intrusive memories, avoidance, and sleep problems after a frightening medical event.
  • Find people who understand. The Society of Critical Care Medicine lists recovery and support resources for patients and families.
  • Protect your job while caregiving. If you are eligible, the federal Family and Medical Leave Act provides up to 12 weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave to care for a spouse, child, or parent with a serious health condition.
  • Get help sooner for the hardest feelings. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 988, any time.

Why This Matters to Me

ICU care saves lives, and it leaves marks on everyone who lives through it, including the people in the waiting room. Families deserve to hear that what they are feeling is common, has a name, and gets better with the right help. If you recognize yourself here, you are not overreacting. You went through something real.
