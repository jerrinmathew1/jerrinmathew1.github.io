Anxiety in Chicago Tech Professionals: Recognition and Evidence-Based Treatment

Tech professionals often normalize anxiety as part of high-pressure work. Recognition and treatment can restore focus and reduce the invisible cost of untreated anxiety.

Who this is for: Engineers, product managers, and founders in Chicago tech. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Anxiety in tech often passes for conscientiousness. The engineer who rereads every pull request three times. The product manager who cannot commit to a decision for fear of choosing wrong. The founder lying awake at 3 a.m. running through problems that will still be there in the morning. In a field that rewards vigilance, it can be hard to see when vigilance has become a problem.

How Common It Is

Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health conditions. Up to a third of people will meet criteria for one at some point in their lives (Bandelow & Michaelis, 2015). They often begin early in adulthood, and many people live with symptoms for years before seeking help.

How Anxiety Shows Up in Tech Work

  • Perfectionism that delays shipping, merging, or deciding
  • Dread before code reviews, demos, or performance reviews
  • Trouble delegating because of worry about what others will miss
  • Late-night rumination about incidents, metrics, or messages
  • Physical symptoms: racing heart, tight chest, headaches, stomach problems
  • Avoidance: muting channels, postponing hard conversations, skipping meetings

Is It Anxiety, or Just the Job?

Some stress is part of demanding work. Anxiety becomes a clinical concern when worry is hard to control, lasts most days for months, and starts to affect sleep, health, relationships, or performance. Brief validated tools such as the GAD-7 can help measure severity (Spitzer et al., 2006). A good evaluation also checks for things that mimic or worsen anxiety, including caffeine, stimulant medications, thyroid problems, sleep deprivation, ADHD, and depression.

What Treatment Can Look Like

Anxiety responds well to treatment. Cognitive behavioral therapy has strong evidence for anxiety disorders, with meaningful benefits over placebo in controlled trials (Carpenter et al., 2018). Medications such as SSRIs and SNRIs are also effective, and a large meta-analysis found that both medication and psychological treatments outperform control conditions (Bandelow et al., 2015). For performance anxiety limited to specific situations, a clinician may discuss other options. The goal is not to take away your focus or your standards. It is to keep fear from running the show.

From the Bedside

In the ICU and on cardiac units, I have cared for many people brought in by chest pain that turned out to be panic. Some were young, fit, and convinced they were having a heart attack, and their relief at a normal workup was quickly followed by embarrassment. There is nothing embarrassing about it. Panic and anxiety produce real physical symptoms, and they are among the most treatable conditions in psychiatry.

When to Get Help

If worry or panic has lasted more than a few weeks and is affecting your sleep, health, or work, an evaluation can help sort out what is going on. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
