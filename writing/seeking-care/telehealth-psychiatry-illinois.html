Telehealth Psychiatry in Illinois: Pros, Cons, and What to Expect

For Chicago and Illinois professionals seeking psychiatric care, understanding your options and what to expect helps you make informed decisions about treatment.

Who this is for: People in Chicago and across Illinois trying to find the right mental health care. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Telehealth has become one of the main ways people in Chicago and across Illinois reach a psychiatric clinician. For a busy professional, a 30-minute visit from a quiet office can be the difference between getting care and putting it off for another year. Remote care is not right for every situation, though, and knowing its strengths and limits helps you use it well.

Does It Work?

Large reviews of the research have found that psychiatric care by video produces outcomes comparable to in-person care for many conditions, including depression and anxiety, with high satisfaction among patients (Hilty et al., 2013; Bashshur et al., 2016). Assessments done over video agree closely with those done face to face (Chakrabarti, 2015). In a randomized trial of people with depression in rural clinics, care coordinated through telemedicine actually produced better outcomes than the same model delivered on site, likely because it was easier to keep people engaged (Fortney et al., 2013).

Where It Shines

  • Access. No commute, and no limit to people who live near a particular office.
  • Continuity. Busy weeks, travel, and schedule changes interrupt care less often.
  • Privacy. For some people, a visit from home feels less exposed than a waiting room.
  • Follow-up. Medication check-ins and therapy sessions translate especially well to video.

Where It Falls Short

  • Physical exam. Blood pressure, heart rate, weight, and movement cannot be checked directly, which matters for some medications.
  • Crises. Severe depression with suicidal thoughts, mania, or psychosis usually calls for in-person and sometimes urgent care.
  • Technology. A poor connection or a shared, noisy room can make a visit harder than it needs to be.

How to Get the Most From a Video Visit

  • Find a private space where you will not be overheard. A parked car works in a pinch.
  • Use a stable connection, and test your camera and microphone before the visit.
  • Sit facing a light source so your face is visible.
  • Keep a list of your medications, doses, and questions within reach.
  • If you have a home blood pressure cuff or scale, have recent readings ready.
  • Know where you are physically located during the visit. Clinicians need to know, because licensing depends on the state you are in at the time of the visit.

From the Bedside

In emergency and critical care, I have learned to value what I can see and measure directly. I have also seen how many people arrive in crisis after months of untreated symptoms, often because care was hard to fit into their lives. Telehealth is not a substitute for an exam when one is needed, but for many people it turns psychiatric care from a quarterly ordeal into something that fits around a workday, and that consistency is often what makes treatment work.

When to Get Help

If a video visit ever reveals that you need to be seen in person, that is the system working as it should, not a failure of telehealth. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
