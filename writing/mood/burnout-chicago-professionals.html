Burnout and Depression in Chicago Professionals: When High Performance Becomes Unsustainable

Burnout and depression are not weakness or poor coping. They are signals that something structural needs to change. For Chicago professionals, recognition and evidence-based treatment restore agency and wellbeing.

Who this is for: Professionals in Chicago and across Illinois dealing with low mood, exhaustion, or burnout. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Burnout in high-performing professionals often starts quietly: fatigue that a weekend does not fix, cynicism about work that once felt meaningful, and a growing sense that nothing you do makes a difference. Over time, it can become hard to tell where burnout ends and depression begins. Both deserve to be taken seriously.

What Burnout Is

Researchers describe burnout in three parts: exhaustion, cynicism or detachment from work, and a reduced sense of effectiveness (Maslach & Leiter, 2016). The World Health Organization classifies it as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical diagnosis, meaning it arises from chronic workplace stress that has not been managed successfully.

Burnout tends to grow from mismatches between a person and their job in six areas: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values (Maslach & Leiter, 2016). That is why rest alone rarely cures it. If the mismatch remains, the exhaustion returns.

Stress, Burnout, and Depression

  • Stress is a response to demands. It usually eases when the demands do.
  • Burnout is the long-term result of stress without recovery, centered on work.
  • Depression affects every part of life, not only work, and includes loss of pleasure, changes in sleep and appetite, guilt, and sometimes hopelessness.

The overlap between burnout and depression is large. A review of the research found that many people meeting criteria for severe burnout also meet criteria for depression, and that the two can be hard to separate (Bianchi et al., 2015). This matters because depression usually needs treatment of its own.

Why It Matters for Your Health

Burnout is not only unpleasant. A systematic review of prospective studies linked it with later insomnia, depressive symptoms, type 2 diabetes, high cholesterol, heart disease, and more sick leave (Salvagioni et al., 2017).

What Helps

Interventions aimed at the individual, such as skills for managing stress, and those aimed at the organization, such as changes to workload and scheduling, both reduce burnout. Programs that combine the two tend to produce longer-lasting results (Awa et al., 2010). In practice, recovery usually involves:

  • An honest look at which of the six mismatches is driving the problem
  • Protecting sleep and restoring time that belongs to you
  • Screening for depression, anxiety, and alcohol use, which often travel with burnout
  • Treating depression directly when it is present, with therapy, medication, or both
  • Changes at work where possible, from boundaries on availability to a different role

From the Bedside

Healthcare has some of the highest burnout rates of any field, and I have watched it happen to excellent nurses: first the joking stops, then the extra effort, then they leave. What has helped most, in my experience, is rarely a single vacation. It is changing something real about the work and having someone notice early. That applies just as much to a trading floor or a law firm as it does to an ICU.

When to Get Help

If exhaustion has spread beyond work, or you have lost interest in things you used to enjoy, an evaluation can clarify whether depression is part of the picture. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
