When anxiety starts affecting leadership

Anxiety rarely announces itself in a leader. It shows up as control.

Anxiety can be highly adaptive in a leadership role. It helps a person anticipate risk, prepare for difficult conversations, and identify problems before they arrive.

Excessive anxiety does something different, and the difficulty is that it does not look like fear from outside. It looks like management style.

How it actually presents

The anxious leader tends to over-check. Decisions already made get revisited. Reassurance is sought repeatedly, sometimes from several people in sequence until someone gives the right answer.

Difficult conversations get postponed. Delegated work gets inspected more closely than the delegation implied. The worst plausible outcome is treated as the likely one. Decisions wait because certainty has not arrived, and certainty is not going to arrive.

At work, anxiety usually wears the costume of control, perfectionism, irritability, overpreparation, avoidance, and constant availability.

NIMH describes anxiety disorders as conditions involving excessive fear or anxiety that interferes with daily functioning. In leadership, the interference is frequently invisible to the person because every individual behavior has a professional-sounding justification.

The organizational cost

This matters beyond the individual, which is what distinguishes anxiety in a leader from anxiety in anyone else.

Micromanagement transmits the anxiety downward. Teams managed anxiously become cautious, stop raising problems early, and begin optimizing to avoid criticism rather than to do good work.

Delayed decisions have compounding costs. Avoided conversations mean performance problems persist and the eventual conversation is far harder. Over-checking consumes the leader’s attention, which is the scarcest resource in the organization.

Leadership requires tolerating uncertainty

No executive has complete information. Every significant decision is made with a gap between what is known and what would be needed to be sure.

Tolerating that gap is not a personality trait some people happen to have. It is a capacity, and anxiety erodes it. If the need for certainty becomes unrealistic, leadership becomes slower and more rigid at precisely the moments that call for speed and flexibility.

The distinction worth drawing

Careful is not the same as anxious. Some of the best executives are extremely thorough, and thoroughness is not a symptom.

The useful markers are whether the checking terminates, whether the preparation is proportionate to the stakes, whether the leader can act without full information when the situation demands it, and whether any of this is producing distress, sleep loss, or physical symptoms.

A leader who prepares exhaustively for a board meeting and then sleeps well is thorough. A leader who prepares exhaustively, cannot sleep, rereads the deck at midnight, and asks three people whether it was fine is describing something else.

What treatment is aiming at

The objective is not to remove all anxiety. A leader without any would be a different and probably worse problem.

It is to prevent anxiety from making decisions on the leader’s behalf — so that a decision to wait is a judgment about the situation rather than a response to discomfort, and a decision to check again reflects genuine risk rather than a need for relief.
