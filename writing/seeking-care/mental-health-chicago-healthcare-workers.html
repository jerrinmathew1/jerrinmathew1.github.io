Mental Health Resources and Care for Chicago Healthcare Workers

For Chicago and Illinois professionals seeking psychiatric care, understanding your options and what to expect helps you make informed decisions about treatment.

Who this is for: Physicians, nurses, and other healthcare workers in Chicago. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

Healthcare workers spend their days looking after other people, often in the worst moments of those people's lives. The work is meaningful, and it takes a toll that the profession has been slow to acknowledge: long and irregular hours, constant exposure to suffering and death, moral distress when the system does not allow the care you know a patient needs, and a culture that treats exhaustion as a badge of honor.

What the Numbers Show

Burnout among US physicians rose sharply during the COVID-19 pandemic. In a national survey, the share of physicians reporting at least one symptom of burnout climbed from 38% in 2020 to 63% in 2021 (Shanafelt et al., 2022). Nurses and other clinicians reported similar strain.

The consequences can be serious. A large study of US death records found that both nurses and physicians died by suicide at higher rates than the general population (Davis et al., 2021). Among physicians who died by suicide, problems at work were more often a contributing factor than among other people who died by suicide (Gold et al., 2013). These are not reasons for alarm so much as reasons to take early signs seriously.

Why Clinicians Wait

  • Identity. "I take care of people. I am not supposed to need care."
  • Licensing fears. Worry that treatment will have to be reported or will affect privileges. Many licensing boards and hospitals have revised their questions to focus on current impairment rather than past treatment, and asking a confidential resource about your specific situation is reasonable.
  • Self-treatment. Clinicians know medications and can be tempted to manage symptoms themselves, which removes the outside view that good care depends on.
  • Time. Rotating shifts and twelve-hour days make ordinary appointment hours hard to use.
  • Culture. Units that normalize skipping breaks and working sick also tend to normalize suffering in silence.

What Good Care for Healthcare Workers Addresses

  • Burnout and compassion fatigue, and how they differ from depression
  • Moral injury, the lasting distress of being part of care that conflicts with your values
  • Trauma symptoms after critical incidents, codes, and patient deaths
  • Sleep disruption from night and rotating shifts
  • Alcohol and substance use, including use of medications to sleep or stay alert
  • The practical side: schedules, boundaries, and returning to work after time away

From the Bedside

I have worked as an EMT, a licensed practical nurse, a house supervisor, and an ICU nurse, and I have seen colleagues carry things home that no one at work ever asked about: the code that went wrong, the family that blamed them, the night that ran sixteen hours. The healthiest teams I have worked on made it normal to say "that one was hard" out loud. The rest of us need the same permission when the hard things start to pile up, and we deserve clinicians who understand the work without needing it explained.

When to Get Help

If you have noticed changes in your sleep, mood, drinking, or patience at work that have lasted more than a few weeks, that is reason enough to talk to someone. Many hospitals also offer confidential employee assistance programs. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
