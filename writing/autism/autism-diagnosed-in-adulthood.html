Autism Diagnosed in Adulthood: Why Capable People Get Missed

The diagnosis was not missed because it was mild. It was missed because you were good at compensating.

Who this is for: Adults in Chicago and across Illinois who are autistic, or wonder whether they might be. Based on published research and years at the bedside in emergency and critical care. The practice opens in 2028 — join the notification list to hear when I am taking patients.

Jerrin Mathew, BSN, RN
Psychiatric-Mental Health Nurse Practitioner Student
3 min read · Updated November 2026

A growing number of adults are being assessed for autism in their thirties, forties, and later — often after a child is diagnosed, or after a career change removes the structure that had been holding everything together. Many describe the same reaction: relief, followed by grief for the decades spent assuming they were simply bad at something everyone else found easy.

Why It Gets Missed

The picture most clinicians were trained on was drawn from children, mostly boys, with obvious difficulties in language or daily function. Adults who are verbally fluent, academically successful, and employed did not match it, so the question was never asked.

Intelligence allows people to learn social rules explicitly that others absorb intuitively. That works — it is how many autistic professionals function well — but it is effortful in a way that is invisible from outside.

What looks like ease is often a rehearsed performance running in the background all day.

What It Often Looks Like in a Professional Adult

  • Exhaustion after social interaction that seems out of proportion to what happened
  • A strong need for routine and predictability, and real distress when plans change at short notice
  • Sensory sensitivities — open-plan offices, fluorescent light, background noise — that colleagues do not seem to notice
  • Deep, sustained focus on areas of interest, often the foundation of professional success
  • A history of being told you are blunt, intense, or hard to read, when you were being direct
  • Recurrent anxiety or depression that has been treated without anyone asking why

Why the Diagnosis Matters as an Adult

It is not usually about treatment in the medication sense. It is about accuracy. People who have spent years in therapy for anxiety, or on antidepressants for depression, sometimes find that the underlying driver was never named, and that understanding it changes what actually helps.

It also changes self-interpretation. Many adults have built an explanation of themselves around personal failure. A more accurate explanation is often the most useful thing an assessment provides.

What an Adult Assessment Involves

A detailed developmental history, ideally including someone who knew you as a child; structured interview; and careful attention to what is better explained by something else — ADHD, social anxiety, trauma, or personality factors overlap substantially and need to be distinguished rather than assumed.

Online questionnaires can prompt the question. They cannot answer it.

What the Research Shows

Many autistic adults, especially women and people with strong language and intellectual abilities, were missed as children because older diagnostic ideas did not match how autism showed up in them (Lai & Baron-Cohen, 2015). Camouflaging contributes: autistic adults describe working hard to appear typical, at a real cost to their wellbeing (Hull et al., 2017). Co-occurring conditions are common, including anxiety, depression, and ADHD (Lai et al., 2019), and are often what bring adults to care in the first place.

From the Bedside

Hospitals are hard environments for anyone, and harder for people who process sensory and social information differently. I have seen how often adults are treated for anxiety or depression for years without anyone asking about the pattern underneath. A late diagnosis does not change who someone is. It often changes how they understand themselves, and what support finally makes sense.

When to Get Help

If you have long felt that you experience the world differently, and anxiety or burnout keep returning despite treatment, an adult autism assessment may be worth considering. Your primary care clinician is a good first call, and can screen for depression and anxiety and refer you on. If you are having thoughts of suicide, or feel unsafe, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline, any time, or go to the nearest emergency department.
